---
title: Company-by-Company Preparation Guide
summary: What Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple, IBM, Oracle, and Tesla interviews generally emphasize, and how to adjust your preparation accordingly.
---

Interviews at big tech companies share a common framework (see the [Interview Process](/tracks/mulakat/mulakat-sureci) lesson); the differences show up in **emphasis**. This guide shows you where to shift your focus in the final weeks of your preparation.

> **Important note.** Interview processes vary from company to company, team to team, country to country, and year to year. The information here consists of **general trends** based on companies' public descriptions and experiences widely shared by candidates; it is not a guarantee. Always ask your recruiter about the steps of your own process, and read the company's official careers page. Recruiters are usually open about the format of the process; don't hesitate to ask.

## At a glance

| Company | Key emphasis | Preparation focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google (Alphabet) | Algorithmic problem solving, general ability | Algorithms, complexity analysis, thinking out loud |
| Meta | Speed and fluency, practical design | Solving medium problems quickly and cleanly |
| Amazon | Leadership Principles | Behavioral stories (STAR), then coding and design |
| Microsoft | A balanced mix that varies by team | Core algorithms, design, collaboration |
| Apple | Team-specific domain depth | The tech stack of the team you're applying to |
| IBM | A wide range of roles, assessment tests | Core programming, the domain knowledge the role requires |
| Oracle | Varies by team; data and systems fundamentals | Databases, SQL, concurrency, algorithms |
| Tesla | Hands-on, domain-specific skills | Being able to explain your past projects in depth |

## Google (Alphabet)

**General structure.** The process usually starts with a phone/online technical interview and continues with a series of onsite interviews: mostly coding, system design depending on seniority, and a behavioral interview the company calls "Googleyness and leadership." The interviewers' written feedback is evaluated together by a **hiring committee**; team matching is often a separate step.

**Emphasis.** Solid knowledge of algorithms and data structures. Not just a working solution: you're expected to weigh alternatives, analyze complexity correctly, and write clean code. Questions usually don't map directly onto a memorized pattern; they call for a combination of known techniques.

**Preparation.** The entire [Algorithms](/tracks/algoritmalar) track, especially graphs, trees, and dynamic programming. Get used to writing code in a plain editor without autocomplete. For the behavioral interview, prepare stories about handling ambiguity and collaboration.

## Meta

**General structure.** The onsite loop usually consists of two coding interviews, one design interview (system design or product architecture, chosen based on your role), and one behavioral interview.

**Emphasis.** **Speed.** In coding interviews, two problems in 45 minutes are often expected, so you need to be able to write medium problems without hesitation or mistakes. The behavioral interview focuses on conflict resolution, moving fast, and a focus on impact.

**Preparation.** Review the common patterns (arrays, strings, trees, graphs) **while timing yourself**. Build the habit of verifying your code by tracing it by hand, without running it. For frontend roles, JavaScript fundamentals and component design are also asked: see the [JavaScript](/tracks/javascript) and [React](/tracks/react) tracks.

## Amazon

**General structure.** The process often starts with an online assessment (OA). In the onsite loop, every interview includes both technical and behavioral questions. One of the interviewers is the **"Bar Raiser,"** who comes from outside the hiring team and is responsible for upholding the bar.

**Emphasis.** Amazon's public **Leadership Principles**: Customer Obsession, Ownership, Dive Deep, Deliver Results, and others. Behavioral questions carry noticeably more weight than at other companies and are spread across every interview. Answers are expected to be concrete, data-driven, and told in the first person ("I").

**Preparation.** Read the Leadership Principles on the company's own page and prepare at least one, preferably two, stories for each principle: the [STAR method](/tracks/mulakat/star-yontemi). Include numbers and results in your stories. For senior roles, scalability and operational resilience stand out in system design; object-oriented design questions are also frequently reported.

## Microsoft

**General structure.** The process **varies noticeably by team**. Typically, a screening interview is followed by a loop of four or five interviews; one of the last interviews is often conducted by a senior manager.

**Emphasis.** A balanced mix: algorithms, design, and collaboration. The company culture puts the "growth mindset" front and center; openness to feedback and eagerness to learn are what behavioral questions look for. On teams in the .NET ecosystem, depth in C# and .NET is naturally a plus.

**Preparation.** Be solid on the core data structures (arrays, linked lists, trees, strings). The [C#](/tracks/csharp) and [.NET](/tracks/dotnet) tracks are directly useful for teams that use these technologies. Research the team's product and tech stack in advance.

## Apple

**General structure.** Hiring is largely **team-based**; there's no centralized, uniform process. Interviews are usually conducted by members of the team you'd be working with, and the type of questions is shaped by the team's work.

**Emphasis.** **Domain depth** and attention to detail. Alongside general algorithm questions, expect practical questions specific to the team's domain (e.g., iOS, systems software, performance, data pipelines). You need to be able to defend the technical decisions in your past projects in detail.

**Preparation.** Use the requirements in the job posting as your preparation plan. Be ready for in-depth questions about every line of every project on your résumé. Being able to show your interest in the products and the user experience with concrete examples helps.

## IBM

**General structure.** IBM hires across very different lines of business (cloud, consulting, research, enterprise software), and the process varies by role. At the entry level, the process often includes online assessments covering coding and general aptitude, followed by technical and behavioral interviews.

**Emphasis.** Core programming skills, problem solving, and the technology knowledge the role requires. In client-facing roles, communication and collaboration are evaluated noticeably.

**Preparation.** Easy and medium algorithm problems, core data structures, and the tech stack of the role you're applying for. Familiarity with cloud and enterprise system concepts (distributed systems, databases) is useful.

## Oracle

**General structure.** The process varies by team. Loops for the cloud infrastructure (OCI) teams are described as similar to the coding- and system-design-heavy loops of other big tech companies.

**Emphasis.** Alongside algorithms, **database and systems fundamentals**: SQL, indexing, transactions, concurrency, operating system concepts. The Java ecosystem is widespread.

**Preparation.** In addition to your algorithm preparation, review writing SQL queries, how indexes work, isolation levels, and multithreaded programming.

## Tesla

**General structure.** The process varies from team to team and is less standardized than at other companies. Software roles span a wide range, from vehicle software to manufacturing systems, and from internal tools to autonomous driving.

**Emphasis.** **Hands-on skill and hard problems solved in the real world.** The focus is on what you concretely built in past jobs, which technical decisions you made and why, and how you overcame obstacles. Being able to deliver results under tight deadlines and uncertainty stands out.

**Preparation.** Be ready to explain your two or three hardest projects from start to finish, with technical detail: what the problem was, which options you considered, and what the result was. Review the fundamentals specific to the role's domain (embedded systems, data pipelines, web services). Classic algorithm questions can come up too; don't skip the basic preparation.

## What stays the same no matter the company

1. **The fundamentals are shared.** Data structures, algorithms, and clean code are asked everywhere. At least three quarters of your preparation is company-independent.
2. **Communication is scored.** Conveying your thinking out loud is an evaluation criterion at every company: [Problem-Solving Method](/tracks/mulakat/problem-cozme-yontemi).
3. **Have your stories ready.** There's a behavioral interview in every loop.
4. **Ask about the process.** Ask your recruiter how many interviews there will be, what types they are, and which language you can use. This information exists to be shared with you.
5. **Research the company.** Knowing its products, the team's work, and the values the company has published strengthens both your questions and your answers.

## Summary

- The difference between companies lies more in emphasis than in content: algorithmic depth stands out at Google, speed at Meta, the Leadership Principles at Amazon, and domain depth and past work at Apple and Tesla.
- At Microsoft, IBM, and Oracle, the process varies noticeably by team; research the team and the role.
- The information here consists of general trends; confirm your own process with your recruiter.
