---
title: The Interview Process at Big Tech Companies
summary: The stages from application to offer, what each stage measures, and how the level system changes the expectations.
---

Big tech companies' interview processes are surprisingly similar to one another. The details vary by company, team and level, but the skeleton is the same. Knowing that skeleton tells you what to prepare for and how much.

> Processes change over time. What's described here gives you the general structure; always confirm the current steps for the position you're applying to with your recruiter and on the company's official careers page.

## Stages

| Stage | Duration | What's measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application / referral | — | How well your résumé fits the position |
| Recruiter call | 20–30 min | Motivation, a summary of your experience, logistics (visa, location, timing) |
| Online assessment (OA) | 60–90 min | 1–3 algorithm questions under time pressure |
| Technical phone screen | 45–60 min | Live coding with an engineer (1–2 questions) |
| Onsite / virtual onsite ("loop") | 4–6 interviews | Coding, system design, behavioral |
| Evaluation | 1–2 weeks | Combining the feedback, the level decision |
| Offer and negotiation | — | Compensation package, team matching |

Not every company runs every stage. Some go straight to a phone screen instead of an OA; some do team matching before the offer.

## What's inside the onsite loop

A typical loop consists of four or five interviews:

- **Coding (2–3 interviews).** Each is 45 minutes, usually one medium question or one easy and one medium. Working code isn't the only expectation: you're expected to clarify the problem, explain your approach, state the complexity and test your own code.
- **System design (1–2 interviews).** Decisive for senior positions. For new-grad positions it's often absent or replaced by object-oriented design.
- **Behavioral (1 interview).** Topics like conflict, ownership and failure are asked about through your past experience. At Amazon, this topic is spread across all the interviews.

## What interviewers write down

After the interview, the interviewer fills out written feedback. These forms differ between companies, but four axes are almost always there:

1. **Problem solving.** Do they resolve ambiguity? Can they consider more than one approach and weigh the trade-offs?
2. **Coding.** Is the code correct, readable and idiomatic for the language?
3. **Verification.** Do they find edge cases on their own, and can they trace through the code line by line?
4. **Communication.** Do they think out loud, and can they make use of hints?

The most important takeaway: **reaching the correct solution silently can score worse than reaching an almost-correct solution while thinking out loud.** The interviewer can't read your mind; they only write down what you say and what you write.

## The level system

Companies divide engineers into levels. The names differ, but they roughly correspond like this:

| Scope | Google | Meta | Amazon | Microsoft |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New grad | L3 | E3 | SDE I (L4) | 59–60 |
| Mid-level | L4 | E4 | SDE II (L5) | 61–62 |
| Senior | L5 | E5 | SDE III (L6) | 63–64 |
| Staff and above | L6+ | E6+ | Principal (L7+) | 65+ |

As the level goes up, the questions don't get harder; **the expectations change**:

- **New grad / mid-level:** The weight is on coding. It's enough to show you can solve a well-defined problem cleanly.
- **Senior:** The system design and behavioral interviews become decisive. They look for examples where you can say "I took an ambiguous problem and led a team to a result".
- **Staff and above:** The conversation is about technical decisions that affect multiple teams, strategy and scope of impact.

The same loop can produce offers at different levels. A candidate who applies for a senior position and is weak in the design interview may get a mid-level offer ("down-leveling").

## How the decision is made

At most companies, the decision isn't one person's. Interviewers write their feedback independently, and then a committee or a debrief meeting reads all the feedback together. This has two practical consequences:

- **One bad interview usually isn't the end.** If the other interviews are strong, it can be made up for. That's why it matters to regroup after an interview that went badly and start the next one fresh.
- **"Not bad" isn't enough.** When a committee is undecided, the default answer is usually no. Your goal is to leave a strong enough impression that at least one or two interviewers clearly advocate for you.

## Rejection is part of the process

Good engineers get rejected often too; the process deliberately treats a false positive (a bad hire) as more expensive than a false negative. At most companies you can reapply after 6–12 months, and your previous attempt isn't held against you. Treat every interview as a rehearsal for the next one, and right after the interview, write down the questions and the places where you got stuck.

## Summary

- The process: recruiter → (OA) → phone screen → onsite loop → committee → offer.
- You're scored on four axes: problem solving, coding, verification, communication.
- As the level rises, the weight shifts from coding to design and impact.
- The decision is made collectively; a single interview neither saves you nor sinks you.
