---
title: The Behavioral Interview and the STAR Method
summary: Preparing for past-experience questions ("Tell me about a time…"). The STAR structure, building a story bank, common topics and the most frequent mistakes.
---

The behavioral interview rests on the assumption that "past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior". The interviewer doesn't ask what you would do but **what you did**: "Tell me about a time you had a disagreement with a teammate."

Most engineers underestimate this interview and walk in unprepared. Yet candidates who do well in the technical interviews can be rejected here, and at senior levels this interview is often what determines the level.

## The STAR structure

| Letter | Meaning | Content | Share of time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **S** | Situation | Context: the team, the project, the problem | ~15% |
| **T** | Task | **Your** responsibility and the goal | ~10% |
| **A** | Action | The steps **you** took and why | ~55% |
| **R** | Result | The measurable impact and the lesson you took away | ~20% |

An answer should take 2–3 minutes. The most common mistake is spending most of the time describing the situation; the valuable part for the interviewer is the **action**.

### Example

**Question:** "Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline."

> **Situation:** The rewrite of our checkout page was two weeks behind schedule, three weeks before a major campaign.
>
> **Task:** I was the technical lead on the project; I had to ship it without missing the campaign date and without increasing the risk of payment errors.
>
> **Action:** First I split the remaining work into "required for the campaign" and "can wait", and together with the product manager we postponed about a third of the scope. Then I took on the riskiest piece myself, the payment provider integration, and steered two teammates toward more independent tasks. I launched the new page behind a feature flag, first to five percent of traffic, so that if something went wrong we could roll back within minutes.
>
> **Result:** We moved to full traffic four days before the campaign. Throughout the campaign, the payment error rate was thirty percent lower than with the old page. We finished the postponed work in the next two sprints. After this experience, we made it a team rule to start every large project with a gradual rollout plan.

What to notice in this answer: "I" language (the personal contribution is clear), the **reasoning** behind the decisions, a measurable result and the lesson learned.

## The story bank

You can't prepare a separate story for every question, and you don't need to. Pick **6–8 strong stories** from your past; each can be adapted to several questions. Make sure you cover these topics:

| Topic | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| A hard technical problem | "Tell me about the hardest technical problem you've faced." |
| Disagreement | "A time you disagreed with a teammate or your manager?" |
| Failure | "A project that failed or a mistake you made?" |
| Leadership and initiative | "A time you went beyond your job description?" |
| Ambiguity | "How did you make progress on work where the requirements weren't clear?" |
| Time pressure and prioritization | "How did you choose between several urgent tasks?" |
| Feedback | "A piece of tough feedback you received and what you did afterward?" |
| Developing others | "A time you mentored someone or moved the team forward?" |

Write each story **as bullet points** under the STAR headings (not as a script to memorize) and rehearse it out loud. A memorized script sounds artificial; bullet points let you tell it naturally.

## What sets a good answer apart

- **"I", not "we".** "We redesigned the system" hides your contribution. Don't take credit for the team's success, but say clearly what **you** did.
- **Specifics and numbers.** Instead of "I improved performance", say "I brought p95 response time down from 800 milliseconds to 200". If you don't remember the exact number, give an approximate one; don't make it up.
- **Reasoning.** **Why** you chose that path and which alternative you ruled out is as valuable as what you did. That's what's expected of senior candidates.
- **Self-reflection.** What would you do differently today?

## Failure and disagreement questions

What these questions look for isn't perfection but **maturity**.

- Talk about a **real** failure. Disguised praise like "I'm too much of a perfectionist" doesn't convince the interviewer.
- Take responsibility; don't blame circumstances or other people.
- Put the weight on what came after: what did you learn, and **how did your behavior change**?
- In disagreement stories, don't badmouth the other side. Describe how you tried to understand their point of view, how you turned to data, and how in the end you committed to the decision (even if it wasn't the one you wanted).

## Be ready for follow-up questions

The interviewer will dig into your story: "Why did you choose that path?", "How did the team react?", "What exactly was your role?". That's why you should tell **real** stories; made-up stories fall apart at the second question.

## "Do you have any questions for us?"

You always do. Not asking questions reads as a lack of interest. Good questions draw on the interviewer's own experience:

- "What does an engineer on your team need to have done in their first six months to be considered successful?"
- "What was the hardest technical decision you made recently?"
- "How do your code review and deployment processes work?"

Save topics like salary and time off for the recruiter.

## Summary

- Build your answers with STAR; spend most of the time on the **action**.
- Prepare a bank of 6–8 stories, write them as bullet points and rehearse them out loud.
- Use "I" language, numbers and reasoning.
- For failure questions, give a real example and highlight what you learned.
- Have a few genuine questions ready to ask the interviewer.
