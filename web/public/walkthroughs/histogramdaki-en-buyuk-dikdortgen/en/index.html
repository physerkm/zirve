Largest Rectangle in Histogram

With a monotonically increasing stack, we find how far each bar can extend to the left and right in O(n) time: when a shorter bar arrives, we pop the taller ones, compute their area, and extend the left boundary backward.

The idea in three steps

  1. 1

    Keep increasing heights on a stack

    Keep (Start, Height) pairs on the stack. As long as the bars keep getting taller, the rectangles can keep growing to the right.

  2. 2

    Pop() when a shorter bar arrives

    If the new bar is shorter than the one on top, the taller bar on top has hit its right boundary; pop it and measure the area prevHeight * (i - prevStart).

  3. 3

    Extend the left boundary backward

    The short bar can extend to the left through the taller bars that were just popped, so move its left boundary back with start = prevStart.

Why is it so efficient?

Each bar is pushed once and popped once

The inner while loop may look scary, but each Pop() permanently removes a bar that was pushed earlier. With n pushes and at most n pops in total, the running time is O(n).

Why do we move the left boundary (start) back?

For example, in the array [2, 1, 2], the bar of height 1 is at index 1, but since the 2 to its left is taller, a rectangle of height 1 can start at index 0. start = prevStart makes sure we don't lose this information.

What happens to the bars left on the stack after the loop?

When we reach the end of the array, the bars left on the stack never hit a shorter bar to their right, so their right boundary is the end of the array (heights.Length).

Why is brute force O(n²)?

Walking left and right separately for every bar scans the whole array at each step for increasing or equal-height arrays, which takes billions of operations for an input of 100,000 elements.
