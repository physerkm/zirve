Car Fleet

Once we sort the cars from the one closest to the target to the one farthest away, we can tell in a single pass whether each car catches up with the fleet ahead of it just by comparing arrival times.

The idea in three steps

  1. 1

    Compute the arrival time

    Compute, as a decimal, how many hours each car would take to reach the target if nothing were in its way: (target - pos) / speed.

  2. 2

    Start from the car closest to the target

    Sort the cars by position from right to left (largest to smallest). Nobody can slow down the car at the front.

  3. 3

    Larger time means a new fleet

    If the car behind has a time greater than leadTime, it can't catch the one ahead and starts a new fleet; if it is less than or equal, it joins the fleet ahead.

Why is it so efficient?

No need to find the meeting point

Instead of working out at which mile two cars meet, we only compare their arrival times at the target. If the unobstructed arrival time of the car behind is shorter than or equal to the one ahead, it is sure to catch up before the road ends.

Why do we sort from right to left?

If we went left to right, we couldn't know in advance whether a car would be slowed down by a slower car further ahead. When we start from the car closest to the target, the arrival time of the fleet ahead (leadTime) is already final.

Why is double division required?

Integer division treats 4 / 3 and 3 / 3 as the same value (1) and makes a car that can't catch up look as if it did. (double)(target - pos) / speed keeps the real fractional time.

Time and space complexity

Sorting the n cars by position takes O(n log n), and counting the fleets in a single loop afterward takes O(n). The pass finishes with just the leadTime variable, without even keeping an extra stack.
