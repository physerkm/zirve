Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock

Think of every day as a possible selling day. Step through how we find the maximum profit (best) in a single pass by keeping the lowest price so far (lowest) up to date.

The idea in three steps

  1. 1

    How much would I make if I sold today?

    If you sell on a given day, the best day to buy is the cheapest day before it. Knowing the lowest price so far is enough.

  2. 2

    Keep the cheapest price up to date

    As you scan the array from left to right, record the lowest price you've seen so far with lowest = Math.Min(lowest, price).

  3. 3

    Compare against the best profit

    Each day, look at the difference price - lowest and update the record profit with best = Math.Max(best, price - lowest).

Why is it so efficient?

One pass instead of a double loop: O(n) time

Trying every (i, j) buy–sell pair takes O(n²). But for each selling day, only the smallest number before that day matters; we track it in a single pass in O(n) time.

Constant memory: O(1) space

Instead of storing all past days, we use just two integer variables (lowest and best). No matter how big the array is, the extra memory stays O(1).

Why is highest − lowest wrong?

In the array [7, 1, 5], the highest is 7 and the lowest is 1, but we can't buy at 7 and sell at 1. The single-pass solution naturally preserves time order: the buy always happens before (or on the same day as) the sell.

Finding a new low keeps the old record

In the example [2, 4, 1], lowest = 1 on the last day, but best still keeps the value 2 from the 2→4 trade. Finding a cheaper buy price doesn't erase the best profit from the past.
