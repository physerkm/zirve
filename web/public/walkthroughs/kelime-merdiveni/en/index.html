Word Ladder

Follow step by step, through the layers of a breadth-first search (BFS), the fewest words it takes to get from beginWord to endWord when you change a single letter at each step.

The idea in three steps

  1. 1

    Put the dictionary in a HashSet

    We move the words in wordList into a HashSet. If endWord is not in this set, the target can't be reached; we return 0 right away.

  2. 2

    Find neighbors with 26 letter swaps

    Instead of comparing against every word in the dictionary, we put each letter from 'a' to 'z' at every position of the word and look it up in the set in O(1).

  3. 3

    Find the shortest path with BFS layers

    Every neighbor word found is removed from the set and added to the queue. The number of the layer where we first reach endWord is the length of the shortest transformation.

Why is it so efficient?

Why don't we compare pairs?

With N = 5000 words in the dictionary, comparing every pair of words takes O(N² · M), that is, 25 million operations. Replacing each letter of a word of length M with 26 letters and looking it up in the HashSet takes only 26 · M lookups.

Why is removing from the set enough?

BFS scans layer by layer, with all edges of equal weight. The moment we first reach a word, we have already reached it by the shortest path; removing the word with wordSet.Remove keeps us from putting it in the queue again in later layers.
